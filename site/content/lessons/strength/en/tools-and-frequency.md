# Bands, chains and extreme frequency

Evaluate the resistance profile and transfer of a tool.

## Tools need a mechanism {#tools-and-frequency-1}

Bands and chains change the resistance profile. The authors note that raw lifters often fail in a different portion of the movement from equipped lifters, so importing an equipped method without examining the limiting region can reduce transfer. Supramaximal top-end loading can also add substantial fatigue and alter execution. A tool is neither magical nor automatically useless; its function must match the goal.

Unstable implements can limit the force that can be applied, and kettlebells provide resistance without special exemption from overload requirements. They may suit some tasks while becoming too light or awkward for another. Ask what is being trained, what constrains loading and whether the relevant competition capacity improves.

## Elite success does not isolate a cause {#tools-and-frequency-2}

The “Bulgarian” high-frequency example involves weightlifting, selected athletes, particular training phases and unusually extensive recovery support, including the source’s discussion of drug use. These conditions confound the claim that daily maximal lifting caused all the success or should suit every powerlifter.

The authors allow high-frequency training in selected situations and short concentrated-loading blocks, but reject universal adoption. Daily technical practice is not the same as a psychologically maximal attempt every day. Compare the whole dose, exercise demands and recovery environment. A successful athlete demonstrates that a method can coexist with success, not that every element caused the result.
