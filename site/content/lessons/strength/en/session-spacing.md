# Spacing and SRA mistakes

Count and spacing are separate scheduling variables.

## Same count, different arrangement {#session-spacing-1}

Three lower-body exposures on Monday, Wednesday and Friday differ from Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday despite equal frequency. The clustered version can impair the later sessions and leave a long subsequent gap. Roughly distributed work is a useful starting point. Uneven spacing can still work if session difficulty is adjusted and the schedule intentionally permits recovery.

The source also explains why perfect equal spacing is not always ideal: a longer lower-demand period in the microcycle may help fatigue management. Frequency and arrangement are therefore separate decisions. Write both the number and the spacing of demanding exposures in the plan.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 229–242 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Do not overinterpret the curve {#session-spacing-2}

Too much frequency can prevent intended overload; too little can lose useful practice; clustering creates both problems in one week. Yet waiting for perfect recovery of every system is also a mistake. The training goal determines which response matters most at a given point, while fatigue management protects the longer sequence.

Soreness alone is neither a complete readiness measurement nor a reason to ignore a problem. The model does not tell a learner to train through injury or identify the cause of pain. Likewise, planned overreaching belongs near a planned easier period, not automatically at the first week of a long accumulation. Use measured performance and context to interpret the schedule.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 229–242 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).
