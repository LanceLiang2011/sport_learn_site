# Avoid randomness and rigidity

Explain both consistency and change.

## Too little variation {#variation-errors-1}

Using the identical load, sets and repetitions indefinitely can neglect changing dose needs and different phase goals. Using only competition movements can limit targeted development. The authors also argue that never removing a competition movement can reduce opportunities to renew its stimulus; this is their programming position rather than a requirement to remove every lift on a fixed schedule.

Retaining an exercise that is still productive is different from retaining it because of a slogan. A training log should explain why it stays. Likewise, a change should solve a problem or serve a new phase, not satisfy a calendar without evidence.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 265–277 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Too much variation {#variation-errors-2}

Four failure modes are non-specific variants, insufficiently challenging variants, phase-inappropriate variants and excessively frequent changes. A balance drill that limits force is not a replacement for substantial strength overload. A high-fatigue isolation movement is not automatically appropriate during peaking.

The source distinguishes moderate daily undulation within a common strength emphasis from extreme weekly alternation among incompatible priorities. A session using six repetitions and another using four need not violate directed adaptation. The criticism concerns trying to maximize every goal simultaneously. Inspect actual workload and objectives rather than assuming that a label such as DUP guarantees either success or failure.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 265–277 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Recap

- Explain both consistency and change.
- Program labels cannot substitute for examining workload and goals.
