# Why the recoverable dose changes

Work capacity and recovery both constrain MRV.

## Capacity meets recovery {#mrv-context-1}

Work capacity is the workload that can be performed; recovery capacity is the ability to restore readiness and adapt afterward. MRV is constrained by both. Better conditioning for the task may expand work capacity, but inadequate sleep or dietary restriction can still lower the recoverable dose. A dose tolerated in one phase cannot be transferred unchanged to another load range.

Proximity to failure matters even when sets, repetitions and load match. Repeatedly taking every set to failure can lower the volume that remains sustainable. Track actual repetitions and effort alongside load, and revise an estimate of MRV when lifestyle or training conditions change.

## Why lifts and lifters differ {#mrv-context-2}

The authors discuss lift type, range of motion, muscle mass, absolute strength, experience, fiber characteristics, sex-related averages and technique. They generally regard deadlifts as more fatiguing than squats, and bench presses as less fatiguing, while recognizing that individuals differ. These are tendencies, not fixed conversion factors between exercises.

A stronger or larger lifter can impose a larger absolute disturbance despite matching another lifter’s percentage of 1RM. More efficient technique can reduce unnecessary demands. Population averages cannot determine an individual’s exact recovery schedule, and fiber type cannot be read reliably from a single repetition test. The useful implication is to keep separate response records for each major lift.

## Phase-specific limits and recovery tools {#fatigue-decision-map}

| Context | What the source wants preserved | What to monitor |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | Continued muscle development | Repeated work capacity and recovery context; size is difficult to measure acutely |
| Strength | Heavy force-producing performance | Comparable repetition performance and execution |
| Peaking | Stable maximal-load expression | Successful specific repetitions and technique |
| Active rest | Rapid fatigue reduction | Whether low demands actually allow recovery |

The book defines MRV over a useful time frame, especially the mesocycle, rather than as a single bad set. Its illustrated progression begins below the average limit, approaches it and may briefly exceed it before a planned deload. This does not mean every early week should already be maximal. Nor does it mean that a learner can measure muscle loss from one flat workout: many suggested field signs are indirect, nonspecific proxies.

Rest days also have a psychological role. Travel, preparation and the association of the gym with hard work can themselves be demands; a light session does not replace every benefit of a day away. Active rest can be directed toward technique learning, flexibility limitations and appropriately managed rehabilitation without becoming a new overload block. The source gives about two weeks in one discussion, 2–4 weeks elsewhere and 1–3 weeks in its summary table. These varying estimates reinforce the principle and individual response, not false calendar precision.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 120–160, 179–182, 343 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).

- Work capacity and recovery both constrain MRV.
- Matched percentages do not guarantee matched fatigue.
