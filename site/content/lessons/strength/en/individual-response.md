# Same principles, different quantities

Individualization adjusts quantities within shared principles.

## Inter- and intra-individual differences {#individual-response-1}

Inter-individual differences describe how people respond differently to the same training. Intra-individual differences describe how one person’s response changes over time. Training experience, sleep, nutrition and stress can change even while genetics remain constant. A program that worked two years ago is therefore evidence, not a permanent personal identity.

The source places most useful adjustments in five areas: MRV; fatigue and fitness decay; development status and goals; exercise selection; and technique. This makes individualization manageable. It usually changes the amount and arrangement of relevant training rather than inventing entirely new physiological principles for each person.

## Fitness and fatigue can decline differently {#individual-response-2}

A lifter may lose fatigue quickly while retaining fitness, or experience the reverse. That distinction influences taper aggressiveness and spacing. Neither pattern can be inferred from body size alone. Previous comparable training and competition records help build a working hypothesis.

The book’s beginner, intermediate and advanced labels refer to development and needs, with years given only as rough examples. Beginners usually need broad technical and muscular development. Intermediates can build productive strengths. Advanced lifters may invest more in limiting factors, detailed peaking and longer fatigue management. Goals such as changing weight class can alter these priorities at any stage.

## Match the adjustment to the evidence {#individual-decision-map}

| Evidence or difference | Candidate variable | Keep stable enough to interpret |
|---|---|---|
| A recoverable dose is too low or high | Set count and session size | Exercise and effort conventions |
| A progression is too abrupt | Starting point and increase size | Goal and comparison movement |
| A variant has poor fit or transfer | Exercise selection | Enough other workload context |
| A technique conflicts with proportions | Specific technique details | Task standard and controlled execution |
| Fitness and fatigue decline differently | Taper timing and reduction | Comparable outcome records |

The same average recoverable volume does not imply the same progression. One athlete may need a lower starting point and larger increments; another may tolerate a higher start but require smaller jumps. The source recommends discovering this through repeated blocks, not choosing the largest increment as a statement of motivation.

Preference can guide selection among effective options, while unfamiliarity can make an initially useful technique feel awkward. Distinguish tolerable learning from an unsuitable movement, and judge the result after enough practice rather than the first exposure. A useful personal plan is therefore a revisable model supported by records. It should not become an untestable claim that every disappointing result proves the athlete is an exception.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 308–336 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).
