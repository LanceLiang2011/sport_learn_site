# Reading the SRA model

Recovery and adaptation overlap.

## A sequence, not a biological stopwatch {#sra-model-1}

A training stimulus disrupts current capacity. Recovery restores previous function; adaptation improves capacity beyond the initial baseline. These processes overlap rather than waiting neatly for one another. A drawn SRA curve is a teaching model, not a direct measurement of an individual’s readiness.

A second large stimulus too early can interfere with the desired response; waiting too long can allow useful adaptations to decline. Planned overreaching is an exception that depends on later recovery. The book’s diagrams help explain this logic, but they do not establish a universal day on which every lifter should train again.

## Several systems respond to one session {#sra-model-2}

The source considers technical learning, muscle growth and architecture, neural force production, and connective tissues. A light practice session emphasizes skill; a high-volume session emphasizes muscle development; very heavy work emphasizes maximal-force skill and related demands. All systems still receive some exposure.

The book suggests technical processes tend to run faster than muscle development, with some neural-force and connective-tissue processes slower. Its precise time-course examples are broad author estimates. Waiting for every tissue to be completely recovered before any practice would make skill exposures too infrequent. Instead, identify the dominant objective and use lighter work and periodic fatigue reductions to manage competing time scales.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 186–209 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Recap

- Recovery and adaptation overlap.
- A single session has several adaptation-specific consequences.
