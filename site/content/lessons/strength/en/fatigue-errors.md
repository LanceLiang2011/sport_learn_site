# Recover enough without retreating from training

The goal is productive training, not maximal fatigue or maximal rest.

## Under-management {#fatigue-errors-1}

The source criticizes chronic excessive volume, constantly lifting too heavy, copying elite routines, matching a stronger partner and refusing deloads or active rest. A deload that remains overloading does not perform its intended function. Social comparison is especially misleading when recovery resources, training age or drug exposure differ.

The response is not to celebrate discomfort but to identify the mismatch. Has technique deteriorated? Is comparable performance falling repeatedly? Does the current dose leave room for the next required exposure? Lowering the dose can improve the training sequence even when the reduced workout appears less impressive in isolation.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 161–185 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Over-management {#fatigue-errors-2}

Avoiding all fatigue can prevent sufficient overload. Too-frequent deloads, work far below the needed effort or replacing every planned session with rest can shrink productive training time. Conversely, complete time off can remove useful practice without necessarily being the most efficient way to preserve adaptations; the appropriate tool depends on the actual problem.

The aim is not zero fatigue every day. It is enough recoverable stimulus to keep improving, periodic reductions that restore productive work, and careful distinction between normal transient fatigue and a persistent problem. The chapter’s physiological narrative supports this programming principle but does not provide a validated fatigue calculator.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 161–185 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Recap

- The goal is productive training, not maximal fatigue or maximal rest.
- Repeated unplanned recovery breaks warrant revisiting the dose.
