# Choose the scale of recovery

Recovery tools differ in both duration and training demand.

## Four tools, distinct purposes {#recovery-tools-1}

Rest days contain no training. Light sessions reduce demands while preserving movement practice. Deloads provide a longer reduction across a microcycle. Active rest follows a long macrocycle, emphasizing sustained low demands rather than a new accumulation. These tools vary both duration and stimulus; none is defined merely by feeling easier.

The source’s examples often reduce both volume and load, sometimes to around half of a prior overload day, but it explicitly calls its percentages educated coaching estimates. “50% volume” and “50% intensity” must be interpreted carefully: halving sets and load together quarters volume load, rather than halving it. State whether a rule refers to sets or tonnage.

## Plan first, adjust with evidence {#recovery-tools-2}

Planned recovery gives a structure; autoregulation accommodates departures from expectation. The authors favor modest changes to sets or load when recovery is better than expected, and targeted light sessions when fatigue is unexpectedly high. Repeated emergency deloads suggest the starting workload estimate needs revision.

Adjustments must preserve the competition date and the time required for later phases. Extending a successful hypertrophy phase by two weeks can steal the strength preparation that follows it. Track the accumulation-to-deload ratio, but do not treat it as a score that overrides actual recovery. A useful log records the reason for each change and the response in the next comparable session.

## Recap

- Recovery tools differ in both duration and training demand.
- Autoregulation should refine a plan and respect later phases.
