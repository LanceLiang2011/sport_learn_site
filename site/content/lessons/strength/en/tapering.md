# Tapering: preserve fitness, reduce fatigue

A taper removes fatigue while preserving competition ability.

## Peaking is not just resting {#tapering-1}

Peaking develops the ability to express strength in maximal competition lifts. The taper is the final reduction of demands intended to remove fatigue while retaining that ability. In a simple conceptual model, preparedness improves when useful fitness remains and fatigue declines. This is a relationship model, not a validated equation that predicts kilograms on meet day.

The source organizes tapering into normal or intentionally elevated work, lower volume with maintained substantial loading, and finally reductions of both volume and loading. Optional extra rest follows. Functional overreaching is not mandatory; its added stimulus and recovery requirements must fit the athlete and the available time.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 292–299, 341–342 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Individual tapering and uncertain precision {#tapering-2}

The book’s taper categories use body mass, performance classification and experience, leading to rough one-, two- or three-week examples. Larger, stronger, experienced lifters are described as often needing longer fatigue reduction while retaining fitness longer. These categories reflect the source’s coaching model, not a universal scoring system.

Lift-specific fatigue also matters: the authors generally reduce deadlift demands earlier than bench demands. A successful previous taper is useful evidence, but changed strength, body mass or recovery can alter the next response. Record session timing, workload and competition outcomes, then revise cautiously rather than changing many factors at once. The competition rules and actual calendar constrain the plan.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 292–299, 341–342 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## Source taper examples and their limits {#taper-decision-map}

| Part | Source example | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Normal peaking volume, or optional 1.5–2 times it | An optional overload is not obligatory |
| 2 | Approximately 50–90% of normal peaking volume, substantial load retained | Reduce work while preserving specific exposure |
| 3 | Approximately 50% volume and 50% loading of normal peaking work | Later reduce both demands; specify each denominator |

The source uses three rough categories. It assigns one, two or three points to body-mass bands (under 165 lb, 165–220 lb, over 220 lb), experience bands (under 3 years, 3–6 years, over 6 years) and its named performance classes. Totals of 3–4, 5–7 and 8–9 points suggest approximately one-, two- and three-week taper examples. These historical performance labels belong to the source and are not a current federation standard or a validated personal calculator.

The short category allows overlapping ranges for its parts rather than three fixed durations that must simply be added. Larger categories spread the parts more evenly. A learner should understand the construction, then inspect actual response; choosing a category does not guarantee a better meet. No part of this table computes a safe maximal attempt or a personalized workload.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 292–299, 341–342 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).
