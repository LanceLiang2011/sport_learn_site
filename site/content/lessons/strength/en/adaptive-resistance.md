# Why a useful stimulus can become stale

Variation should serve a relevant adaptation.

## Negative feedback and adaptive resistance {#adaptive-resistance-1}

Negative feedback limits a biological response as its result accumulates. The authors use it to explain adaptive resistance: repeated exposure to the same stimulus produces progressively smaller improvements. This differs from simple fatigue; a lifter can recover from a dose yet gain less from repeating it indefinitely.

Variation changes training variables to renew useful stimulation and distribute demands. It can include exercise, volume, load, repetition range, frequency or velocity. This is not evidence that a muscle needs to be surprised each day. The mechanism is an organized change in relevant demands, bounded by specificity and the time needed for directed adaptation.

## Several forms of staleness {#adaptive-resistance-2}

The chapter applies its model to technique, hypertrophy, neural force production and connective tissues. An exercise change can alter recruitment and lines of loading. A lower-volume strength phase can offer a different stimulus after a long hypertrophy phase, while a higher-repetition block can interrupt constant near-maximal work.

The book’s cellular explanations and proposed resensitization mechanisms are hypotheses and simplifications rather than measurements available in the gym. Do not use soreness as a direct growth meter: a novel movement can be sore without proving that it produces superior long-term results. Compare repeatable performance and tolerance over a suitable block.

## Recap

- Variation should serve a relevant adaptation.
- Fatigue and adaptive resistance are related but distinct ideas.
