# Audit a complete plan

A macrocycle should have connected purposes and explicit preservation goals.

## Work backward from the task {#macrocycle-audit-1}

For a fictional future meet, reserve time for competition-specific preparation and a taper. Before that, allocate enough strength-focused time to develop force using the available muscle. Earlier preparation can develop muscle and work capacity. After competition, include a deliberately lower-demand active-rest period before another build.

This sequence is an original worked planning exercise based on the book, not a prescription with guaranteed results. It deliberately omits an individualized number of weeks or loads. The important test is whether every block has a purpose, preserves what the next block needs and fits the fixed event date.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 337–343; synthesis of Chapters 2–9 (printed page labels; PDF page index is label minus 1).

## A seven-question audit {#macrocycle-audit-2}

1. Does the work transfer to the target task?
2. Is the stimulus sufficient and progressively demanding over time?
3. Can the sequence be recovered from?
4. Do session dose and spacing fit the intended adaptation?
5. Is variation purposeful and stable enough to evaluate?
6. Does one block prepare rather than erase the next?
7. What evidence supports individual adjustments?

A failed answer directs revision. Do not solve a lack of relevant work by adding a sophisticated taper, or solve excess fatigue by adopting a more exotic program name. Keep the smallest useful record: actual work, effort, comparable performance, recovery context and the reason for changes.

## An original planning worksheet {#cycle-decision-map}

| Block | Write before beginning | Review before transition |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | Needed muscles, recoverable dose, progression | Is work capacity improving and the needed muscle emphasis retained? |
| Strength | Target movements, loading reference, adequate volume | Can force expression improve without losing the base? |
| Peaking | Regulation execution, specific exposure, fatigue constraints | Is technique stable as load rises? |
| Taper | Named reference dose and planned reductions | Is fatigue declining while ability remains? |
| Active rest | Low-demand objective and next-cycle preparation | Are readiness and identified movement needs improving? |

For a worked arithmetic check, imagine one weekly squat exposure changes from 5 × 5 × 120 kg (3,000 kg·repetitions) to a proposed light session of 5 × 10 × 75 kg (3,750 kg·repetitions). The bar became lighter while this volume proxy rose by 25%. That does not prove fatigue rises by 25%, but it disproves the claim that the proxy decreased. To design a genuine reduction, state which variables are intended to fall and recompute them.

The source tables also distinguish working sets and light sets and allow compounds to contribute to several muscles. This is why a total cannot be interpreted without its counting convention. Reuse the worksheet when a future course introduces another programming model: retain its particular definitions instead of silently blending incompatible set-count or intensity conventions.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 148–149, 337–343 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).
