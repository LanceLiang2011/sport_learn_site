# No magic programs

Program names do not cause adaptation.

## Neither slogans nor paralysis {#program-claims-1}

“Just lift” can hide missing principles; endless attention to small details can hide the same problem. The source recommends informed planning followed by consistent execution and critical review. A minor toe-angle or accessory-order debate matters less than whether the required range, stimulus and recoverable workload are present.

Random day-to-day training makes progression and response difficult to interpret. Autoregulation is different: it changes a plan for a stated reason while preserving its objective. Write down the change so the outcome can inform the next decision. Effort and thought complement one another.

## Judge the actual program {#program-claims-2}

The book ends by rejecting both the belief that programming never matters and the belief that a prestigious name creates results. Beginners can improve under many imperfect plans; advanced lifters often need more precise matching. Many structures become useful after a small adjustment to dose, selection or progression, but no label bypasses the principles.

For a new claim, ask: What adaptation is proposed? What is the relevant comparison? What dose and population were involved? What costs were measured? What uncertainty remains? The course teaches the source’s reasoning while separating its coaching opinions from measured facts. Further reading should be consulted in its original context rather than treated as a list of automatically proven claims.

## A claim-evaluation map {#claim-decision-map}

| Claim | Missing question |
|---|---|
| A famous athlete uses this tool | What selection, other training and recovery factors coexist? |
| More cardio feels easier to recover from | Did the desired strength outcome also improve? |
| More mobility is always better | What required position is currently unavailable or uncontrolled? |
| A lower class is always more competitive | What happened to strength, health and the relevant scoring context? |
| Greater body mass means greater useful capacity | How much is muscle, and what leverage or comfort changes occurred? |
| Every hard program is equivalent | Do specificity, dose, progression and recoverability actually match? |

The chapter’s twelve themes can be organized into tools (bands/chains and gym implements), dose fashions (extreme frequency and cardio), reasoning errors (oversimplification and detail obsession), task fit (mobility and incompatible goals), body-mass tradeoffs (unnecessary descent or excessive gain), and planning claims (random sessions and magic programs). Each should be examined by the same framework rather than replaced with the opposite slogan.

**Worked critique:** “This tool produced a personal record, so everyone should use it.” The record may be real, but the inference changes population, goal and comparison. Ask whether load, practice, taper or a previous adaptation also changed. Explain why the resistance profile should transfer. A plausible mechanism supports a test; it does not prove universal superiority. Conversely, a limitation for powerlifting does not make a tool useless for every other sport or recreational goal.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 344–371 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).

## Recap

- Program names do not cause adaptation.
- Use principles, actual outcomes and explicit uncertainty to evaluate claims.
