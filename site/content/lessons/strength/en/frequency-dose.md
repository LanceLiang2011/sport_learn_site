# Frequency depends on dose

More sessions can redistribute or increase workload; distinguish the two.

## Changing the size of each exposure {#frequency-dose-1}

Frequency cannot be evaluated independently of session volume, loading and effort. Splitting a weekly dose among more sessions reduces the dose per session; adding sessions without reducing the others increases the weekly demand. These are different experiments even if both are called higher frequency.

The book identifies two boundaries. Extremely small sessions may fail to provide sufficient overload; extremely large sessions may add recovery cost without proportionate adaptation. Infrequent training also risks losing practice or adaptation between exposures. Several schedules can therefore work within a useful middle region. Select one that permits sufficient high-quality work and is feasible to repeat.

## Different needs, different schedules {#frequency-dose-2}

Technical proficiency, muscle size, strength, session volume and the particular lift all influence scheduling in the source. Its examples commonly give bench practice more opportunities than heavy deadlifting. The point is individual dose-response, not that an exercise name dictates a fixed number of days.

The chapter gives broad phase-related frequency ranges and notes that the book’s own recommendations vary across discussions. Do not treat a range as a requirement detached from weekly workload. An original comparison is 12 weekly sets arranged as 6 + 6 versus 4 + 4 + 4. Weekly set count matches, but quality, local fatigue and practical fit may differ. Observe those outcomes before declaring one schedule superior.

## Read curves without inventing measurements {#sra-comparison-map}

| Model quantity | Interpretation | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Technical SRA | Practice can change coordination relatively quickly | A guaranteed daily practice requirement |
| Muscle SRA | Recovery and protein remodeling occur after loading | Net tissue gain from a single measured signaling response |
| Neural-force SRA | Force-producing ability can be affected beyond the session | An exact “CNS recovery day” from subjective feeling |
| Connective-tissue SRA | Some structural remodeling has a long time course | A safe dose or injury diagnosis from the drawing |

The source’s hypertrophy diagram overlays fractional synthetic rate (FSR) with its performance curve. An elevated synthesis rate is a process measure; net muscle change also depends on breakdown and the longer context. Muscle pennation angle—the fiber direction relative to the tendon—is discussed as another exercise-sensitive architectural feature. Neither can be inferred from a soreness rating.

The figures show why timing solely for a fast process can leave slower ones progressively disturbed, while timing solely for the slowest process can undersupply skill or muscle exposures. The source also offers a middle or phase-biased arrangement. Its larger-session diagrams use stylized doubled magnitudes and durations; that is an illustration, not evidence that doubling sets always doubles adaptation or recovery time. Psychological arousal, the particular lift and adaptation to habitual frequency further complicate the relationship. Use the graph to formulate a hypothesis and the training log to evaluate it.
