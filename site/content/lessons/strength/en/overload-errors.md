# Failure, speed work and workload spikes

Evaluate overload by the training sequence, not a single heroic workout.

## Stimulus and cost are not identical {#overload-errors-1}

The authors question speed work that never reaches a sufficient strength or hypertrophy stimulus. A fast bar is not evidence of adequate overload for every goal. Assistance work should also be demanding enough for its intended capacity; choosing easier movements only to avoid effort can undermine the plan. Track repeated best efforts in comparable conditions rather than relying on memory.

At the other extreme, reaching failure on every heavy set can create disproportionate fatigue and unstable execution. The book generally favors challenging work short of failure for powerlifting. More effort can improve the immediate stimulus while reducing the amount of productive work that can be repeated later.

## Protect the next session {#overload-errors-2}

An unplanned challenge day can exceed the intended dose by so much that the following week becomes recovery rather than training. The initial session may be stimulating, yet its benefit is bounded while its interference with later work continues. Compare the whole sequence, not just how impressive one workout looked.

A useful audit asks: Was this increase planned? Does it serve the current goal? Can the next scheduled session still meet its purpose? Is the progression based on actual response? Psychological commitment matters, but neither fatigue nor pain should be treated as proof of productive overload. Subsequent chapters explain how planned recovery sustains progression.

## Make progression interpretable {#overload-decision-map}

| Observation | First question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bar load rises but sets and reps fall | Did volume load actually rise? | The changed stimulus may serve a different phase |
| Fast repetitions with very light load | Is the intended force or muscle stimulus sufficient? | Maximal intent and low-load speed work are not synonyms |
| A harder assistance exercise | Does it challenge a useful capacity within the recoverable dose? | More systemic disruption is not itself the target |
| A surprise record workout | Can the planned next exposures still be productive? | The single-session gain may cost later opportunities |
| A claimed new personal best | Were range, equipment and effort comparable? | Unmatched conditions can masquerade as progression |

The book favors substantial compound free-weight assistance for its intended powerlifting goals and proposes an ordering from barbells through dumbbells, cables and machines. This is an author preference based on expected overall disruption, not a universally proven ranking of hypertrophic effectiveness. The source itself allows less disruptive isolation work when additional targeted work is needed but systemic recovery is limited. **Target stimulus and whole-body cost must be separated.**

Maximal intent means attempting to apply force strongly; it does not require a fast observed bar. The source argues that apparent benefits of speed days may come from their lower demands and recovery role, rather than velocity practice itself. Track the role of that day before inferring a special mechanism. A log of actual loads, sets, repetitions and execution prevents a remembered “best” from becoming an imaginary progression baseline.

> Scientific Principles of Strength Training, pp. 78–103 (printed page labels and 1-based PDF pages coincide; 0-based PDF index is label minus 1).

## Recap

- Evaluate overload by the training sequence, not a single heroic workout.
- Effort needs a purpose and a recoverable dose.
