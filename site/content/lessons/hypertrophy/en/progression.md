# Progress without exhausting the runway

Combine sets, repetitions, load and RIR using actual response.

## Estimate stimulus conservatively {#progression-proxies}

The authors combine perceived target-muscle challenge, pump and disruption into a raw stimulus magnitude (RSM) score. Each is rated 0–3, giving 0–9 in total. Their MEV estimator regards roughly 4–6 as a useful starting signal, but this is a coaching heuristic, not a validated assay of hypertrophy. RSM is specific to a person and muscle; it should not rank different people.

Use several observations together. No soreness does not demonstrate no stimulus, and a large pump does not demonstrate sufficient tension. Compare equal set counts after allowing time to learn an exercise. A high RSM may also come with excessive fatigue, which the next chapter addresses.

## Progress from response, not enthusiasm {#progression-decisions}

Start near an estimated effective dose. If soreness resolves well before the next exposure and comparable performance improves, modestly adding sets may be reasonable. If recovery occurs just in time and targets are met, hold sets steady. If soreness overlaps and performance falls, investigate fatigue instead of adding work. Performance constraints take precedence over chasing soreness.

Progress load or repetitions enough to stay within the chosen range and effort trajectory. A possible accumulation moves from more RIR toward less, while preserving later training. Do not sharply increase load, sets and failure proximity together just because each can progress separately. For giant sets, use total repetitions plus other comparable straight sets to monitor fatigue; for drop sets and myoreps, record rest and loading conditions.

## Avoid both weak and excessive overload {#progression-errors}

Junk volume adds fatigue without meaningful contribution to the intended adaptation: it may be insufficiently specific, too far from failure under light loads, or beyond useful per-session capacity. More work is not automatically more effective work. Ignoring exercise order, limiting useful range, or prioritizing comfort over the actual target can also reduce stimulus.

At the opposite extreme, chronically very heavy loading, forced repetitions and beyond-failure work can spend recovery resources faster than they add benefit. Stable technique is part of the comparison. If the new record came from shorter range, more momentum or assistance, the log should not treat it as equivalent progression.

## Recap

- Use stimulus and recovery observations together.
- Preserve a productive sequence of sessions instead of maximizing one session.
