# Derive a frequency from observations

Use a repeatable experiment rather than a rigid muscle calendar.

## A feedback loop {#derive-frequency-algorithm}

Begin with a plausible effective session dose near MEV. Observe when soreness and fatigue resolve, then compare performance at the next exposure. If performance cannot meet a comparable baseline, increase recovery or reduce the dose before assuming the muscle inherently needs a long interval. Starting far above MEV can falsely make every muscle seem slow to recover.

A simpler source procedure starts with roughly twice-weekly exposures, progresses within recoverable volume and reviews the completed mesocycle. Consistently early recovery plus good performance can justify testing one more weekly exposure next mesocycle. Just-in-time recovery suggests holding frequency. Overlapping soreness and declining performance suggest less frequency, less dose, or a different exercise.

## An exercise can change the interval {#derive-frequency-example}

**Original comparison:** a trainee recovers promptly from four leg-curl sets but remains disrupted after the same number of demanding hip-hinge sets. Equal set counts do not establish equal recovery demand. Changing the exercise mix can preserve a muscle’s weekly stimulus without forcing every exposure to be equally damaging.

Document session dose, effort, soreness resolution, comparable performance and joint response. Revisit the schedule after the next mesocycle rather than changing everything after one unusual day. A workable real-life calendar with good recovery is preferable to a theoretically symmetric schedule that cannot be followed.

## An original frequency decision table {#derive-frequency-decision-table}

| Repeated pattern | Question to investigate | Possible next experiment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early recovery and improving comparable performance | Is there room for another useful exposure? | Test a modest increase, keeping dose interpretable |
| Recovery just in time and stable targets | Is the present distribution already effective? | Keep frequency while reviewing progression |
| Overlapping soreness with performance loss | Is dose, exercise damage or interval the problem? | Reduce demands or extend recovery |
| Muscle seems ready but joints stay irritated | Are tissue time courses mismatched? | Review loading, method and lower-stress phases |

This original table summarizes the source’s feedback logic without pretending to calculate a biological optimum. The source suggests deriving an interval across 2–5 consecutive exposures, or using a completed mesocycle for the simpler method, rather than drawing a conclusion from one session.

Its theoretical frequency explanation includes muscle size and force, fiber-associated fatigue characteristics, architecture and stretch exposure, sex averages and whole-body demands. For example, a demanding lengthened hamstring hinge can have a different recovery cost from lateral-delt work. Such examples explain why to observe individual response; they do not identify a person’s fiber composition from soreness.

## Recap

- Dose and frequency must be tested together.
- Use repeated patterns and adjust modestly between mesocycles.
