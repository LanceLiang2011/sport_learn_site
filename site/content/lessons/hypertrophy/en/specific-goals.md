# What should training accomplish?

Start with the adaptation you want, then justify each part of the program.

## Directed adaptation {#specific-goals-directed}

[Specificity](term:specificity) asks whether training directly creates the desired adaptation or prepares something that will enable it later. Repeated exposure to a sufficiently consistent stimulus allows technical skill and tissue adaptations to accumulate. Constantly replacing the main exercises or rep ranges can interrupt that process. The authors discuss a possible preparatory-to-myofibrillar sequence of hypertrophy; treat this as their proposed mechanism, not as a measured timetable for an individual.

A light practice squat may support future growth by improving technique even if it is not itself a demanding growth stimulus. This is different from assuming every difficult activity builds the muscles you want. In a training log, give each activity a direct-growth, support, or other-goal role.

## Size and strength overlap, but goals differ {#specific-goals-strength}

A larger muscle can support greater force production, but maximal lifting performance also depends on skill, neural coordination and familiarity with heavy loads. A heavy single may improve expression of strength while providing little total hypertrophy work for its fatigue cost. The book therefore rejects treating a one-repetition record as the main objective of a muscle-growth phase.

Conversely, progressing repetitions with stable technique over several mesocycles can be useful indirect evidence of growth. It is not proof: motor learning and altered range of motion can also raise performance. Compare like with like, and read performance alongside body measurements, photographs and recovery observations over an appropriate timescale.

> Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training, ch. 1, EPUB Text/part0004.xhtml, Conflating hypertrophy and strength training

## Recap

- Justify training by its relationship to the desired adaptation.
- Stable exposure supports learning and adaptation; strength records are not direct muscle measurements.
