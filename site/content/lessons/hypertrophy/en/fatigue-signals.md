# Recognize the cost of training

Fatigue is unavoidable; needless or excessive fatigue is manageable.

## Local, systemic and axial fatigue {#fatigue-signals-types}

Local fatigue affects the trained muscle and associated tissues. [Systemic fatigue](term:fatigue) can reduce readiness and performance in muscles that were not directly trained. Axial fatigue arises from loading the spinal support structures; although local, it can limit many subsequent movements. A fatigued trunk may impair both rowing and squatting even when their primary targets differ.

Useful signals include repeated performance losses, persistent connective-tissue irritation, unusually high perceived effort and reduced performance in otherwise unused muscles. Feeling tired alone is not enough to conclude that overload is impossible. The source gives comparable performance special weight, while acknowledging that perception and performance need interpretation together.

## Fatigue can conceal adaptation {#fatigue-signals-masking}

A productive accumulation phase can create both more fitness and more fatigue. A temporary reduction in performance need not mean muscle was lost, and a performance jump after reduced training need not mean the reduced dose caused superior growth. Testing after comparable recovery conditions is more informative than comparing a fresh first week with a fatigued last week.

However, this does not justify ignoring persistent deterioration. Repeated losses with overlapping soreness or joint irritation are reasons to change the plan. The goal is enough recoverability to continue good training, rather than either permanent exhaustion or a permanently pristine recovered state.

## Recap

- Use several fatigue indicators, emphasizing comparable performance.
- Recovery is sufficient when useful training can continue; zero fatigue is not required.
